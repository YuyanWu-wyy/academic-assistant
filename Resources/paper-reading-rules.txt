# Summarize Research Paper

Produce an evidence-grounded explanation that helps the reader understand not only what the paper says, but why the work was needed, how it works, and whether the evidence supports its claims.

## Workflow

1. Obtain the primary paper and identify its title, authors, venue or preprint status, and version/date. Prefer the paper itself over third-party summaries.
2. Read the abstract, introduction, related work, method, experiments, tables/figures, limitations, and appendix sections relevant to the claims.
3. Trace every central claim to evidence in the paper. Use section, page, equation, figure, or table references when available.
4. Separate statements into:
   - **Paper-stated claim**: what the authors explicitly claim.
   - **Observed evidence**: what the experiments or analysis demonstrate.
   - **Interpretation**: a reasoned explanation or implication.
5. Match the user's language. If the user mixes Chinese and English, default to clear Chinese prose while retaining standard English technical terms in parentheses.
6. Adapt depth to the paper. Explain equations and theory for mathematical papers; emphasize system design, implementation, and efficiency for systems papers; emphasize data, protocol, and validity for empirical papers.

If the full paper is unavailable, summarize only the accessible material and clearly state the resulting limits. Never infer unreported dataset details, results, novelty, or implementation choices.

## Required Analysis

### 1. Problem to Solve

Explain:

- the real-world or scientific problem;
- the specific technical task;
- why the problem matters;
- the paper's scope, assumptions, and success criteria.

Distinguish the high-level motivation from the formal problem definition.

### 2. Existing Methods, Bottlenecks, Field Problems, and Gap

Keep these four layers separate:

1. **Current method families**: organize prior work by approach rather than listing citations.
2. **Method bottlenecks**: identify concrete weaknesses such as accuracy, robustness, scalability, cost, data dependence, generalization, interpretability, or theoretical limitations.
3. **Mainstream field-wide problems**: explain challenges broadly recognized in the area.
4. **Research gap and paper focus**: state precisely what remains unresolved and which part this paper tackles.

Use a compact comparison table when three or more method families can be compared on shared dimensions. Do not accept a claimed gap uncritically; indicate whether it is demonstrated, argued, or merely asserted by the authors.

### 3. Contributions and Novelty

List contributions as verifiable items, normally three to five. Classify each as applicable:

- new problem formulation or theory;
- new model, algorithm, architecture, or system;
- new dataset, benchmark, or evaluation protocol;
- new empirical finding or analysis;
- efficiency, robustness, or practical improvement.

For every item, explain **what is new**, **relative to what prior work**, and **why it matters**. Distinguish the authors' claimed contributions from novelty supported by the related-work comparison.

### 4. Method in Detail

Explain the method from inputs to outputs:

1. Define the task, inputs, outputs, symbols, and assumptions.
2. Give an intuitive overview before technical detail.
3. Walk through the architecture, algorithm, training objective, inference procedure, or proof structure in execution order.
4. Reproduce only the equations needed for understanding. Define every symbol immediately and explain what each term does.
5. Connect equations to implementation: what is optimized, what is learned, what is fixed, and how gradients or updates flow.
6. State computational complexity, memory, data requirements, or theoretical guarantees when reported.
7. Add a small worked example or conceptual analogy when it materially improves understanding.

Render mathematics clearly. For an objective such as

\[
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}(\theta)+\lambda\mathcal{R}(\theta),
\]

explain the role of the task loss, regularizer, parameter set \(\theta\), and trade-off weight \(\lambda\), rather than merely copying the formula.

If the paper has no substantial mathematical method, say so and focus on the actual design or experimental logic.

### 5. Datasets and Experimental Setup

For each dataset, report only what is available:

- name and source;
- data modality and domain;
- task and label type;
- scale, classes, and train/validation/test split;
- collection or preprocessing details;
- whether it is introduced by the paper or reused;
- distribution, bias, privacy, licensing, or leakage concerns;
- its role in training, validation, evaluation, or ablation.

Also summarize baselines, metrics, evaluation protocol, implementation details, compute, and statistical testing when reported. Explain why each metric is appropriate and whether higher or lower is better.

### 6. Results

Report:

- primary quantitative results with metric names and units;
- strongest relevant baseline and absolute/relative improvement when meaningful;
- statistical uncertainty or significance;
- ablations and what they establish;
- robustness, transfer, qualitative, efficiency, and failure-case results;
- negative or mixed findings;
- whether the evidence supports each major claim.

Do not call a result “state of the art” unless the comparison is fair and the paper supports it. Note differences in data, model size, compute, pretraining, or evaluation protocol that weaken comparisons.

## Output Structure

Use this order unless the user requests another format:

1. **Paper at a glance** — citation/status and a two-to-four sentence overview.
2. **Problem being solved**
3. **Existing approaches, bottlenecks, field challenges, and gap**
4. **Main contributions and innovations**
5. **Method explained in detail**
6. **Datasets and experimental setup**
7. **Results and what they mean**
8. **Limitations, open questions, and practical takeaway**

End with a concise bottom line answering: *What changed because of this paper, and how convincing is the evidence?*

## Quality Rules

- Preserve technical accuracy while translating jargon into plain language.
- Cite the primary paper near factual claims when links or citations are requested or available.
- Use exact numbers from tables rather than vague adjectives.
- Mark information as **not reported** when absent.
- Distinguish percentage-point improvement from percent relative improvement.
- Avoid equating correlation with causation or benchmark gains with real-world usefulness.
- Mention reproducibility risks such as unavailable code/data, incomplete hyperparameters, or undisclosed compute.
- Keep summary length proportional to the user's request; do not omit the six required analysis areas merely to be brief.
